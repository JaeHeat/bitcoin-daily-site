Limit Order vs Market Order in Crypto: The Hidden Cost

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 5 min read

Every exchange gives you two buttons. One says Market. One says Limit. Most new traders hit Market, because it is fast and it always works. Nobody tells them that the choice between a limit order vs market order in crypto has a price tag, and that the price tag shows up on every single trade for the rest of their trading life.

I ran a year of trades through both buttons. The gap was bigger than I expected.

What is a market order in crypto?

A market order says one thing. Get me in right now, at whatever price is available. You click, and you are in the trade within a second.

You pay for that speed twice. First, the exchange charges its higher fee, because you took an order that someone else had waiting. Second, you get the worst side of the price. There is always a small gap between what buyers are offering and what sellers are asking. A market order pays that gap every time. In a fast move, the price can also jump before your order lands, so you fill a little worse than the number you saw on the screen.

What is a limit order in crypto?

A limit order says something different. Get me in at this price or better, and I will wait. You name the price. Your order sits in line until the market comes to you.

You get two rewards for waiting. The exchange charges its lower fee, because your order gave other traders something to trade against. And you get exactly the price you asked for, never a worse one.

The catch is real, and I will not hide it. The market does not have to come to you. Sometimes price turns around a few dollars before your order and runs without you. A limit order promises the price. It never promises the trade.

Limit order vs market order fees, in real dollars

On the biggest crypto futures exchanges, the starting fee is about 0.02% for a limit order that waits and about 0.05% for a market order. Those numbers look like nothing. So let us make them dollars.

Say you have a $10,000 account. You risk 1% a trade, and your stop sits 1% away from your entry. That makes the trade itself about $10,000 in size. Every trade has two sides, the way in and the way out.

  • Market in, market out. $5 each way. That is $10 a trade.
  • Limit in, limit out. $2 each way. That is $4 a trade.

Still small. Now remember that you are only risking $100 on this trade. With market orders, a tenth of your risk is gone in fees before the trade does anything.

Then stretch it over a year. I take about 18 trades a month, which is 216 a year.

  • All market orders. About $2,160 a year in fees.
  • All limit orders. About $864 a year in fees.

Same trades. Same wins. Same losses. One trader hands more than a fifth of a $10,000 account to the exchange in a year. The other hands over less than a tenth. Plenty of traders who think they have a losing system really have a winning system with an expensive button. And the more often you trade, the worse it gets, which is one more reason overtrading quietly drains an account.

When to use a limit order and when to use a market order

So limit orders win and market orders are for suckers? No. Each one has a job. Here is how I split them.

  1. Entry is a limit order. I know my price before the trade. If the market does not give it to me, I skip the trade. There will be another one.
  2. Take profit is a limit order. My target is a price I picked in advance. It sits there waiting and fills at that exact number while I do something else.
  3. Stop loss is a market order. This is the one place I pay for speed, and I pay it gladly.

That third rule matters most. When a trade goes wrong, the only job is to get out. A stop that waits for a set price can get skipped when the market falls fast. Price drops straight through your number, your order never fills, and the small planned loss turns into a big unplanned one. Saving $3 in fees is not worth that risk. If you want the full walkthrough on placing one, I covered it in how to set a stop loss in crypto.

Run my own mix through the same math. I win about 7 trades out of 10, and those exit on the cheap limit order. The other 3 exit on the stop at the higher fee. That works out to about $1,058 a year in fees, roughly half the bill of the trader who clicks Market on everything.

The honest downside of limit orders

You will miss trades. Some of them will be the good ones, the kind that leave fast and never look back. It stings to watch a move you planned run away without you.

I take that deal every time. A missed trade costs nothing. Chasing it with a market order costs a worse price, a higher fee, and usually a wider stop to make it fit. Do that a few times a week and you have rebuilt the expensive version of your own system.

The button looks like a small choice. It is not. Pay for speed only when speed is the whole point, which is getting out of a loser. Everywhere else, name your price and let the market come to you.

Common questions

What is the difference between a limit order and a market order in crypto?

A market order fills right now at whatever price is available. A limit order fills only at the price you name or better, so it can wait in line and it might never fill.

Are limit orders cheaper than market orders?

Usually yes. On the biggest crypto futures exchanges the starting fee is about 0.02% for a limit order that waits and about 0.05% for a market order, and a limit order also never fills at a worse price than you asked for.

Should a stop loss be a market order or a limit order?

A market order. When a trade goes wrong the only job is to get out, and a stop that waits for a set price can get skipped in a fast move and leave you stuck in the loss.

Can a limit order fail to fill?

Yes. If price turns around before it reaches your order, the trade leaves without you. A limit order promises the price, never the trade.

How much do market orders cost over a year?

On a $10,000 trade size with 216 trades a year, using market orders both ways costs about $2,160 in fees, while using limit orders both ways costs about $864.
