Bitcoin Inflation Rate, Explained (And Why It Matters)

By Josh Molnar · October 2026 · 5 min read

Every government on earth can print more money whenever it wants. Bitcoin cannot. The bitcoin inflation rate is baked into the code, it ticks down on a schedule everyone can see, and nobody, not a president, not a central bank, not a whale, can change it. Right now that rate sits at roughly 0.8% per year. That is less than half the pace at which gold comes out of the ground, and about a fifth of what the US dollar lost to consumer prices over the past twelve months.

What the bitcoin inflation rate actually means

When people say “inflation” for Bitcoin, they are not talking about the price of groceries. They mean something much simpler. How fast is the total supply of Bitcoin growing?

Every ten minutes (on average), the Bitcoin network creates a new block, and the miner who solves it earns a reward of brand-new coins. That reward is the only way new bitcoin ever enters circulation. Right now the reward is 3.125 BTC per block. Multiply that by roughly 144 blocks a day, 365 days a year, and you get about 164,000 new bitcoin created each year. Against a circulating supply of roughly 19.9 million coins, that works out to approximately 0.8% annual supply growth.

Compare that to the US dollar. Consumer prices in the United States rose about 3.7% over the past year. The Federal Reserve can accelerate or slow that number, but nobody votes on it, nobody can predict it with certainty, and the long-run track record is relentless. One dollar in 1970 buys you about seven cents of stuff today.

The halving schedule keeps cutting it

Here is what makes Bitcoin different from every other asset. The inflation rate does not just sit at 0.8% forever. It gets cut in half, automatically, roughly every four years, in an event called the halving.

The math is simple. When Bitcoin launched in 2009, miners earned 50 BTC per block. Four years later, that dropped to 25. Then 12.5. Then 6.25. After the April 2024 halving, it fell to 3.125. The next halving, expected around 2028, will cut it again to about 1.5625 BTC per block, pushing the annual inflation rate down to roughly 0.4%.

Here is how the rate has fallen after each halving:

  • 2012 halving: inflation dropped from roughly 25% to about 12%
  • 2016 halving: dropped to about 4%
  • 2020 halving: dropped to about 1.8%
  • 2024 halving: dropped to about 0.8%

By the 2030s it will be under 0.2%. By mid-century it will be nearly zero. The last fraction of a bitcoin will not be mined until roughly the year 2140, but by then the new supply will be so tiny it is basically rounding error.

Bitcoin inflation rate vs gold vs the dollar

Gold miners pull roughly 1.5% to 1.7% new gold out of the earth each year relative to all the gold that already exists above ground. That number has stayed in a narrow band for decades. Bitcoin crossed below gold after the 2024 halving, and it will keep pulling away at every future halving.

The dollar does not even have a fixed supply. The Federal Reserve printed trillions during 2020 and 2021. Congress runs deficits that add to the outstanding debt. Nobody can tell you the dollar’s “inflation rate” in advance because nobody controls the full set of decisions that create it.

Bitcoin’s schedule, by contrast, was written on day one. You can check it yourself, right now, in the open-source code. That predictability is the entire point.

Why this matters for holders

A shrinking inflation rate means less new supply hitting the market every year. Miners earn fewer coins, so there is less daily selling pressure from the people who need to sell to cover electricity bills. Meanwhile, demand does not follow the same schedule. It spikes when sentiment shifts, when institutions enter, or when a new cycle picks up steam.

That mismatch between a fixed (and falling) supply and unpredictable (and sometimes surging) demand is why Bitcoin’s price history looks nothing like a gentle upward curve. It looks like long stretches of boredom interrupted by violent moves in both directions. The supply side is a clock. The demand side is a crowd.

None of this guarantees the price goes up tomorrow, next month, or next year. Bitcoin has crashed 50% or more several times in its history even while the inflation rate was falling. But the structural pressure on the supply side only tightens over time, and that is something no other liquid asset on earth can say.

The bottom line

Bitcoin’s inflation rate is now lower than gold’s and roughly one fifth of what the US dollar loses to consumer prices each year. It will be cut in half again around 2028, and again after that, until eventually no new coins are created at all. Whether you think Bitcoin is the future of money or an interesting experiment, the supply math is not an opinion. It is code. And it only moves in one direction.

Common questions

What is Bitcoin’s current inflation rate?

About 0.8% per year as of October 2026. That is the pace at which new bitcoin enters the circulating supply after the April 2024 halving cut the block reward to 3.125 BTC.

How does the Bitcoin halving affect the inflation rate?

Every four years the block reward is cut in half, which cuts the annual supply growth in half too. The next halving, expected around 2028, will push the rate down to roughly 0.4%.

Is Bitcoin’s inflation rate lower than gold?

Yes. Gold’s above-ground supply grows about 1.5% to 1.7% per year from mining. Bitcoin’s rate dropped below that after the 2024 halving and will keep falling at every future halving.

When will Bitcoin stop inflating entirely?

The last new bitcoin is expected to be mined around the year 2140. In practice, the rate becomes so small it is essentially zero well before that, likely by the 2050s.
