Bitcoin Hash Ribbons Explained: the Miner Buy Signal

By Josh Molnar · October 2026 · 5 min read

Bitcoin hash ribbons might be the most honest signal in crypto. It does not care about Twitter opinions, YouTube thumbnails, or what some analyst thinks the chart looks like. It watches miners. When the people who literally power the network start shutting off their machines, this indicator notices. And historically, that moment of miner surrender has been one of the best times to buy Bitcoin.

What are Bitcoin hash ribbons?

Hash ribbons track the total computing power securing Bitcoin (called the hash rate) and compare two simple averages of it: a short one (30 days) and a longer one (60 days). When the short average drops below the long average, it means hash rate is falling. Miners are unplugging. The weakest ones can no longer afford to run their machines, so they quit.

That phase is called miner capitulation. It sounds dramatic, and it is. Mining Bitcoin costs real money: electricity, hardware, cooling, rent. When the price falls far enough that revenue no longer covers those costs, miners have no choice. They shut down.

The buy signal comes later, when the short average crosses back above the long average. That crossover means the bleeding has stopped. The weak miners are gone, the survivors are expanding, and the network is recovering. The indicator was created by analyst Charles Edwards, and it fires only a handful of times per cycle. It is slow, rare, and patient by design.

Why miner stress marks bottoms

Think of it this way. Miners are forced sellers. They earn Bitcoin as a reward for processing transactions, but they pay their bills in dollars. When the price drops, they sell more of their Bitcoin to cover costs. When even selling is not enough, they shut down entirely.

That wave of forced selling is what pushes prices into the final, ugly low that wipes out hope. Once those weak miners are gone, the selling pressure disappears. Supply dries up. And the market quietly turns.

This is not theory. It has played out in almost every Bitcoin cycle. The 2015 bear market bottom, the late-2018 crash to roughly $3,000, the COVID panic in March 2020, the mid-2021 China mining ban, the 2022 collapse below $16,000. In each case, hash ribbons flagged the stress, and the recovery signal arrived near the low.

The track record: 18 out of 21

Since 2012, hash ribbons have fired roughly 21 buy signals. Of those, 18 saw Bitcoin trading higher six months later. That is an 86% hit rate. For context, if you bought Bitcoin on any random day over that same stretch, the odds of being up six months later were closer to 64%.

That gap matters. It means the indicator is not just riding Bitcoin’s long-term upward trend. It is actually picking better entry points than blind luck. Not perfect, but meaningfully better. You can explore the full history on our live hash ribbons chart.

The failures are worth studying too. The August 2022 signal, for example, fired while Bitcoin was still above $20,000. Price dropped further before the real bottom arrived months later near $15,500. The lesson: hash ribbons are a positioning tool, not a precision timer. They narrow the window. They do not promise the exact floor.

What the latest signal means

In early October 2026, hash ribbons flashed a new buy signal. The short-term hash rate average crossed back above the long-term average after a stretch of miner stress. Bitcoin was trading near $82,500 at the time.

There is a caveat worth naming. After the April 2024 halving, the block reward dropped from 6.25 to 3.125 Bitcoin. That means miners earn half as much Bitcoin per block as they did a couple of years ago. Their margins are thinner. Rising mining difficulty and volatile energy costs can distort the hash rate signal in ways the pre-halving data did not face.

That does not make the signal useless. It means you should treat it the way its track record deserves: as one strong piece of evidence, not as the whole case. Combined with other bottom signals (like hash rate recovery trends and the broader bottom checklist), it paints a much clearer picture.

How to actually use hash ribbons

Here is the practical version. Hash ribbons are not a day-trading tool. They fire a few times per cycle, sometimes with months between signals. If you try to trade around them on short timeframes, you will be disappointed.

Instead, treat them as a macro positioning lens. When the signal fires, the question is not “should I buy today?” It is “am I in the zone where accumulation historically pays off over the next 6 to 12 months?”

The answer, 18 out of 21 times, has been yes.

  • Wait for the recovery cross. The capitulation phase (short average below long average) is the stress. The buy signal is the recovery cross back above. Do not jump in during the capitulation itself.
  • Pair it with other signals. No single indicator should run your money. Hash ribbons work best alongside on-chain value measures and sentiment extremes.
  • Think in months, not days. The median time from a hash ribbons buy signal to a meaningful rally is measured in weeks to months, not hours.

Common questions

What are Bitcoin hash ribbons?

Hash ribbons compare the 30-day and 60-day moving averages of Bitcoin's hash rate. When miners capitulate and the short average drops below the long average, then recovers, the crossover back above is a buy signal.

How accurate are hash ribbons buy signals?

Since 2012, roughly 18 of 21 hash ribbons buy signals (86%) saw Bitcoin trading higher within six months. That compares to about 64% for buying on any random day.

Who created the hash ribbons indicator?

Analyst Charles Edwards created hash ribbons to track miner capitulation and recovery as a long-term buy signal for Bitcoin.

Did hash ribbons flash a buy signal in 2026?

Yes. In early October 2026, hash ribbons fired a new buy signal after a stretch of miner stress, with Bitcoin trading near $82,500.

Can hash ribbons predict the exact Bitcoin bottom?

No. Hash ribbons narrow the window to a favorable buying zone but do not pinpoint the exact low. They are a positioning tool, not a precision timer.
