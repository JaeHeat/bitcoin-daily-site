Runfi sustainability model

Can you pay people to walk without the payout mechanism eating itself? This is a 24-month simulation of two ways to try. The numbers on this page are computed live in your browser by the same model that generates the committed CSVs.

payouts(t) ≤ outside_revenue(t)  for every period t

Outside revenue means money that did not come from another user buying in. Subscriptions, brand deals, opt-in partner offers. Not NFT sales to the next cohort. Hold this line and the economy cannot inflate itself to death, no matter how many people join.

What the token version does

The token engine mints rewards at a fixed nominal rate per unit of activity and funds them from new-user buy-in. It is calibrated to the STEPN shape and lands on it: the price falls 99.9%, users peak in month 10, and the protocol mints $7.0M of rewards against $0.37M of real revenue.

The single line that turns a decline into a collapse

The crash is not caused by the emission rate. It is caused by denominating the entry cost in the token being emitted. Sever that one link and change nothing else:

Both still breach the invariant every single month. The difference is only whether users get a gentle disappointment or a wipeout. If you ever do launch a token, never denominate the entry cost in it.

Worth naming: in the fixed-price run the protocol mints $40.1M of rewards against $2.73M of revenue, and the operating company still finishes with $2.15M in the bank. Fees are collected in real money up front while the losses land on token holders. A move-to-earn company can do very well out of a design that is terrible for its users. That is why so many of these launched.

What the revenue-share version does

No token. A fixed share of real outside revenue goes into a pool, split pro-rata by verified activity. Reward per mile floats. The pool cannot be overdrawn, so the invariant holds by construction rather than by discipline.

The constraint is not the payout rule

Sweeping payout share from 5% to 95% breaks nothing. More payout always retains more users and always costs more money. The real wall is scale.

Roughly 100,000 monthly actives to wash its face at a 50% share. This is a customer acquisition and retention problem wearing a tokenomics costume, which is why the answer was never going to be a cleverer emission curve.

Pushing payout per player up

$1.26 a month is not a product, and no emission curve fixes it. What a player earns is set by one identity:

payout_per_player = ARPU × payout_share ÷ earner_share

Payout share is the lever everyone reaches for and it is the weakest of the three. It is bounded by one, so at best it doubles the number. The other two are unbounded, and they multiply.

Four levers move those terms. They are independent, so they stack. Each rung below adds to the one above it, and every rung holds the invariant in all 24 months.

1. Sell something other than attention

Consumer advertising ARPU in fitness tops out near a dollar. Commerce does not: this audience already replaces shoes every few hundred miles. Adding gear at a 10% take and lifting subscription conversion with an earn-it-back hook moves revenue per user from $0.99 to $2.65, and drops break-even from 104k actives to 35k.

2. Stop paying everyone

This is the cheapest lever in the list and it costs nothing. Pay only the players who clear a real activity bar. At a 30% qualification rate the same pool pays 3.3x more per earner, with no extra revenue and no extra risk. The bar is not a trick, it is the product: you are selling a credible claim that the person on the leaderboard actually walked.

3. Pay in something worth more than it costs

Partner credit and gift cards bought below face, or your own margin-bearing gear, land as more than a dollar per dollar spent. At half the payout in kind at a 1.45 face multiple, $4.08 of cost arrives as $5.00 of perceived value. This is Sweatcoin's actual business model, and it is the one lever here that raises the number the player sees without raising the number you pay.

4. Sell to whoever is actually paying for the outcome

This is the unlock, and it is worth more than the other three combined. The player is not the one who benefits most from them exercising. Employers and insurers do, because activity lowers their claims, and they already buy this: corporate wellness and Medicare Advantage fitness benefits are priced per member per month, in the mid single digits to low tens of dollars. That is 5-10x consumer ARPU, and unlike ad inventory it is not capped by attention.

What this costs you. The payer channel is a different company: 6 to 18 month sales cycles, health-data compliance, and outcomes evidence a benefits team will actually sign off on. You would be building a B2B health business with a consumer app attached, not a crypto app. That is the real trade, and it is the honest reason most move-to-earn projects reached for a token instead. The token was the shortcut around a hard enterprise sale.

What the stake game is not. Commitment stakes look like earnings and are not. At a 20 dollar stake and a 72% success rate the winner takes about $6.61, but the expected value to an entrant is minus $0.84, and you would need a 75.2% success rate just to break even against the rake. It is a motivation device funded by users who fail, so it is deliberately excluded from every payout figure on this page.

What actually moves the number

Two levers beat payout share, and neither costs an extra dollar of payout. Below, both runs spend identical marketing money and pay out identical dollars. The only differences are that the app is worth opening with the money switched off, and the same pool is paid as an activity-weighted weekly draw instead of flat micro-payments.

The prize multiplier of 2.2 is the least defensible number in this model. It is set to 1.0 everywhere else, so nothing other than this comparison depends on it.

So can you use a token at all?

Yes, on three conditions, and the answer turns on one distinction: is the token the source of the payout, or just the rail it travels on?

revenue → open-market buyback → distribute

Revenue arrives in cash. The protocol buys the token on the open market. Those bought tokens are what gets paid out. Emission is zero, so the payout is bought, never printed, and the invariant is untouched. Payouts stay quoted in dollars and are bought at the price of the day, so a falling token means more tokens per dollar, not a smaller payout.

That last property is the whole thing. Run the four designs below and the player earns exactly the same amount in every one of them, including the ones whose token goes to zero. The token never touches what anybody earns. It just adds a price you now have to defend.

Condition 1: zero emission, and zero is not a rounding target

Minting on top of the buyback is dilution with no offsetting bid. It is a bright line, not a dial. Printing nothing appreciates the token 88%. Printing 25 cents of token for every dollar bought sends it to -75%, and so does every level above that. There is no safe small amount, because a minted token arrives on the market without a dollar ever having been spent to buy it.

Condition 2: launch at a valuation your day-one revenue can defend

This is what actually killed these projects. Earners sell most of what they receive, so only the part they keep is a net bid. That slice has to absorb everything arriving on the market that nobody bought, and vesting supply is denominated in your own valuation:

max_FDV = buyback × (1 − sell_share) × vest_months ÷ (alloc × unlock_sell)

Size it on the buyback you have in month one, not the one on the pitch deck. Same revenue, same 20% allocation: launched at $2M it appreciates 88% and never dips below coverage 1.02. Launched at $250M it falls 92% with coverage bottoming at 0.07.

Condition 3: never denominate anything users buy in it

The same rule from the very top of this page. It is what turned a decline into a collapse in the minting model and it does not stop being true here.

And then ask what it bought you. A buyback token adds no revenue. Every dollar a player earns came from the same subscriptions, sponsors and payer contracts as before, and the payout would be identical with no token at all. What it adds is a price to defend, a cap table that can sink it, securities exposure, and an ongoing obligation to convert revenue into buy orders. The one thing it genuinely buys is a liquid, borderless payout rail and community ownership. If those are not worth the rest, pay in sats or stablecoin and skip the entire problem, because on the earnings line the model cannot tell the difference.

Run it yourself

Every slider recomputes the full 24 months live. The thing to try first: set revenue per user to zero and watch the payout fall to nothing without the model ever breaching the invariant. That is the difference. A design that cannot pay simply pays less, instead of printing the difference.

What this model does not know

  • Monthly time steps. A weekly pool has within-month dynamics this cannot see.
  • Users are homogeneous. No power users, no geography, no seasonality, and real fitness apps have brutal January cohorts.
  • Retention is a smooth function of last month's payout. Real churn is lumpy and driven by things nothing here represents.
  • Revenue per user is assumed, not observed. Attack this number first, because everything downstream scales with it.
  • No regulation, app store policy on paid rewards, or payout tax treatment. All three are real and none are modelled.
