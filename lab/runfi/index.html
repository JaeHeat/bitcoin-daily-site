Runfi sustainability model

Can you pay people to walk without the payout mechanism eating itself? This is a 24-month simulation of two ways to try. The numbers on this page are computed live in your browser by the same model that generates the committed CSVs.

payouts(t) ≤ outside_revenue(t)  for every period t

Outside revenue means money that did not come from another user buying in. Subscriptions, brand deals, opt-in partner offers. Not NFT sales to the next cohort. Hold this line and the economy cannot inflate itself to death, no matter how many people join.

What the token version does

The token engine mints rewards at a fixed nominal rate per unit of activity and funds them from new-user buy-in. It is calibrated to the STEPN shape and lands on it: the price falls 99.9%, users peak in month 10, and the protocol mints $7.0M of rewards against $0.37M of real revenue.

The single line that turns a decline into a collapse

The crash is not caused by the emission rate. It is caused by denominating the entry cost in the token being emitted. Sever that one link and change nothing else:

Both still breach the invariant every single month. The difference is only whether users get a gentle disappointment or a wipeout. If you ever do launch a token, never denominate the entry cost in it.

Worth naming: in the fixed-price run the protocol mints $40.1M of rewards against $2.73M of revenue, and the operating company still finishes with $2.15M in the bank. Fees are collected in real money up front while the losses land on token holders. A move-to-earn company can do very well out of a design that is terrible for its users. That is why so many of these launched.

What the revenue-share version does

No token. A fixed share of real outside revenue goes into a pool, split pro-rata by verified activity. Reward per mile floats. The pool cannot be overdrawn, so the invariant holds by construction rather than by discipline.

The constraint is not the payout rule

Sweeping payout share from 5% to 95% breaks nothing. More payout always retains more users and always costs more money. The real wall is scale.

Roughly 100,000 monthly actives to wash its face at a 50% share. This is a customer acquisition and retention problem wearing a tokenomics costume, which is why the answer was never going to be a cleverer emission curve.

What actually moves the number

Two levers beat payout share, and neither costs an extra dollar of payout. Below, both runs spend identical marketing money and pay out identical dollars. The only differences are that the app is worth opening with the money switched off, and the same pool is paid as an activity-weighted weekly draw instead of flat micro-payments.

The prize multiplier of 2.2 is the least defensible number in this model. It is set to 1.0 everywhere else, so nothing other than this comparison depends on it.

Run it yourself

Every slider recomputes the full 24 months live. The thing to try first: set revenue per user to zero and watch the payout fall to nothing without the model ever breaching the invariant. That is the difference. A design that cannot pay simply pays less, instead of printing the difference.

What this model does not know

  • Monthly time steps. A weekly pool has within-month dynamics this cannot see.
  • Users are homogeneous. No power users, no geography, no seasonality, and real fitness apps have brutal January cohorts.
  • Retention is a smooth function of last month's payout. Real churn is lumpy and driven by things nothing here represents.
  • Revenue per user is assumed, not observed. Attack this number first, because everything downstream scales with it.
  • No regulation, app store policy on paid rewards, or payout tax treatment. All three are real and none are modelled.
